# Is This a Thing?

Bring back the established name and original source of a concept, rule, or practice. Five moments, in the order the work meets them.

## 1. Whether: is it already a thing?

William F. Ogburn and Dorothy Thomas, [Are Inventions Inevitable? A Note on Social Evolution](https://archive.org/stream/jstor-2142320/2142320_djvu.txt), Political Science Quarterly (1922):

> It is an interesting phenomenon that many inventions have been made two or more times by different inventors, each working without knowledge of the other's research.

> Researches into the histories of science and of inventions reveal a surprisingly large number of instances of multiple but independent origins of inventions. The appendix to this article contains a list of 148 such cases [...]

> Given the boat and the steam engine, is not the steamboat inevitable?

Merton named the independent duplicates. Wikipedia, [Multiple discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_discovery):

> Robert K. Merton defined such "multiples" as instances in which similar discoveries are made by scientists working independently of each other.

Expect the idea to exist already: run the search before the build.

## 2. Name: what is it called?

The search has a name. Wikipedia, [Prior art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_art):

> prior art is generally defined as anything that is made available, or disclosed, to the public that might be relevant to a patent's claim before the effective filing date of a patent application for an invention.

> A "novelty search" is a prior art search that is often conducted by patent attorneys, patent agents or professional patent searchers before a patent application is filed. A novelty search helps an inventor determine if the invention is novel before committing the resources necessary to obtain a patent.

The corpus is everything public. European Patent Office, [Guidelines for Examination](https://www.epo.org/en/legal/guidelines-epc/2026/g_iv_1.html):

> There are no restrictions whatsoever as to the geographical location where the information in question was made available to the public or as to how or in what language it was made available. Nor is any age limit stipulated for the documents or other sources of the information.

A description in hand but no name? Feed the description. OneLook's [reverse dictionary](https://www.onelook.com/thesaurus/beta/):

> This tool lets you describe a concept and get back a list of words and phrases related to that concept. Your description can be anything at all: a single word, a few words, or even a whole sentence.

> We urge you to click on a word to check its definition before using it in your Oscars acceptance speech or honors thesis.

Tools return nothing? Describe it to a human whose work is naming. Wikipedia, [Reference desk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_desk):

> Library users can consult the staff at the reference desk for help in finding information. Using a structured reference interview, the librarian works with the library user to clarify their needs and determine what information sources will fill them.

## 3. Source: where did it start?

A name found secondhand sits inside a claim about its origin. Mike Caulfield, [SIFT (The Four Moves)](https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/):

> Much of what we find on the internet has been stripped of context. [...] In these cases we'll have you trace the claim, quote, or media back to the source [...]

The chasing technique has a name. Claes Wohlin, [Guidelines for Snowballing in Systematic Literature Studies and a Replication in Software Engineering](https://wohlin.eu/ease14.pdf), EASE (2014):

> Snowballing refers to using the reference list of a paper or the citations to the paper to identify additional papers. [...] Using the references and the citations respectively is referred to as backward and forward snowballing.

Walk each source's reference list backward toward the origin; check its forward citations to see who carried the idea on.

## 4. Drift: is the famous name the origin?

Stephen Stigler's 1980 paper, quoted in Wikipedia's [Stigler's law of eponymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigler%27s_law_of_eponymy):

> For "Stigler's Law of Eponymy" in its simplest form is this: "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer."

The same article:

> Stigler attributed the discovery of Stigler's law to sociologist Robert K. Merton, making "Stigler's law" an example of Stigler's law.

Nigel Rees, quoted in Garson O'Toole's [Phrase Origin: Churchillian Drift](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/11/14/churchillian-drift/), Quote Investigator:

> Long ago, I coined the term "Churchillian Drift" to describe the process whereby the actual originator of a quotation is often elbowed to one side and replaced by someone more famous. So to Churchill or Napoleon would be ascribed what, actually, a lesser-known political figure had said. The process occurs in all fields.

Attributions drift to the most famous plausible owner. Keep the earliest appearance you can verify, and treat the celebrity name as a lead to check, not a source to cite.

## 5. Adopt: stand on it or rebuild it?

Isaac Newton, [letter to Robert Hooke](https://newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/OTHE00101) (1676):

> What Descartes did was a good step. You have added much several ways, and especially in considering the colours of thin plates. If I have seen farther, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants.

Five centuries earlier. John of Salisbury, [Metalogicon](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/John_of_Salisbury) 3.4 (1159), trans. Henry Osborn Taylor:

> Bernard of Chartres used to say that we were like dwarfs seated on the shoulders of giants. If we see more and further than they, it is not due to our own clear eyes or tall bodies, but because we are raised on high and upborne by their gigantic bigness.

The drift check works on this page: the famous source was not the origin. The thing you find carries the same offer: stand on it. Wikipedia, [Reinventing the wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinventing_the_wheel):

> To reinvent the wheel is to attempt to duplicate—most likely with inferior results—a basic method that has already previously been created or optimized by others.

Keep the established name so the next searcher finds you; cite the earliest source you verified; build on the found thing rather than duplicate it.
