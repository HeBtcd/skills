# What Are We Assuming

A judgment stands on premises nobody said aloud. Four moments, in the order the work meets them.

## 1. Frame: the request may be the solution

Name the problem separately from the proposed solution before auditing anything. [The XY Problem](https://xyproblem.info/):

> The XY problem is asking about your attempted solution rather than your actual problem. This leads to enormous amounts of wasted time and energy, both on the part of people asking for help, and on the part of those providing help.

> User wants to do X. [...] User asks for help with Y. [...] After much interaction and wasted time, it finally becomes clear that the user really wants help with X, and that Y wasn't even a suitable solution for X.

> The problem occurs when people get stuck on what they believe is the solution and are unable step back and explain the issue in full.

When the request gives only the solution, ask for the problem it was meant to solve. The dig is Taiichi Ohno's five whys (Wikipedia, [Five whys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_whys)):

> The primary goal of the technique is to determine the root cause of a defect or problem by repeating the question "why?" five times, each time directing the current "why" to the answer of the previous "why".

> The key idea of the method is to encourage the troubleshooter to avoid assumptions and logic traps and instead trace the chain of causality in direct increments from the effect through any layers of abstraction to a root cause that still has some connection to the original problem.

Ask why the request is wanted, each why aimed at the answer before it, until the request's problem stands where its solution stood.

## 2. Premises: what the judgment takes for granted

Wikipedia, [Assumption-based planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumption-based_planning), citing RAND:

> RAND defines an assumption as "an assertion about some characteristic of the future that underlies the current operations or plans of an organization."

> Implicit assumptions are not expressed and may go undetected. If implicit assumptions are wrong, this can damage projects.

> Identify assumptions: Collect all assumptions implicit, explicit, primary and derivative, out of the (business) plan.

The argument that hides them is the enthymeme (Wikipedia, [Enthymeme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthymeme)):

> The first type of enthymeme is a truncated syllogism, or a syllogism with an unstated premise.

> The third kind of enthymeme consists of a syllogism with a missing premise that is supplied by the audience as an unstated assumption.

> Aristotle referred to the enthymeme as "the body of proof", "the strongest of rhetorical proofs...a kind of syllogism" [...]

Say aloud the premise the audience was left to supply. The slot where it sits is Toulmin's warrant (Wikipedia, [Stephen Toulmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Toulmin#Toulmin_model_of_argument), The Uses of Argument, 1958):

> Warrant: A statement authorizing movement from the ground to the claim. In order to move from the ground [...] "I was born in Bermuda", to the claim [...] "I am a British citizen", the person must supply a warrant to bridge the gap [...] "A man born in Bermuda will legally be a British citizen".

> The first three elements, claim, ground, and warrant, are considered as the essential components of practical arguments [...]

For each claim in the judgment, name the ground it cites and state the warrant aloud: the bridge between them is where the assumption lives.

## 3. Load: what would have to be true

Roger Martin, [The Strategic Choice Structuring Process](https://rogermartin.medium.com/the-strategic-choice-structuring-process-5e116b12ae1f), on what a strategy assumes:

> Teams tend to disagree intensely on *what is true*, and when the focus is on that disagreement, strategy discussions tend to grind to an unproductive halt. In contrast, they almost always can come to agreement on WWHTBT. The reason is that asking WWHTBT instead of what is true separates the logic of a possibility from the data associated with the logic.

> Be open to any WWHTBT — and put it up on the chart. But after you have sourced all the WWHTBT elements, ask for each of them: "If this single WWHTBT was shown to be not true, would you reject the associated possibility?" If they wouldn't reject, then it is a *nice to have* not a *need to have* and should be removed as a WWHTBT.

> The thing that stops commitment and action are the elements that would have to be true that the team is not confident are true.

List what would have to be true for the judgment to be right, drop every nice-to-have, and mark the need-to-haves nobody is confident in: those carry the verdict.

## 4. Challenge: the judgment is questioned

Kathryn Schulz, [On being wrong](https://singjupost.com/on-being-wrong-kathryn-schulz-transcript/), TED2011:

> It does feel like something to be wrong; it feels like being right. So this is one reason, a structural reason, why we get stuck inside this feeling of rightness. I call this error blindness. Most of the time, we don't have any kind of internal cue to let us know that we're wrong about something, until it's too late.

The challenge is the cue the holder never gets: receive it as evidence about an assumption nobody could feel. Then answer it as Daniel Dennett relays Anatol Rapoport's rules in [Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking](https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/03/28/daniel-dennett-rapoport-rules-criticism/):

> How to compose a successful critical commentary:
>
> 1. You should attempt to re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says, "Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way."
> 2. You should list any points of agreement (especially if they are not matters of general or widespread agreement).
> 3. You should mention anything you have learned from your target.
> 4. Only then are you permitted to say so much as a word of rebuttal or criticism.

Re-express the challenged position until its holder would sign it, list where you agree, state what you learned, and only then answer.

Report the audit as the judgment, the premises it needed, and which of them carry the verdict. When the audit changes the question (the request was a proposed solution, or a load-bearing premise fails), state the new frame before answering on the old one.
